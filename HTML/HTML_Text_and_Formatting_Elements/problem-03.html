India Launches Its Most Powerful AI Supercomputer to Boost National Research

'The government-funded initiative aims to accelerate AI development across universities and research institutions nationwide'

NEW DELHI, INDIA --    In a landmark moment for Indian technology, the Government of India officially launched AIRAWAT-2, the country's most powerful artificial intelligence supercomputer, on 14 August 2026, coinciding with the eve of Independence Day. The supercomputer, housed at the Indian Institute of Technology (IIT), Delhi, is capable of performing over 200 petaflops of computing operations per second, making it one of the top ten most powerful supercomputers in the world.

The initiative is part of India's ambitious National AI Mission (NAIM), which was announced earlier this year with a total budget of ₹10,000 crore allocated over five years. The mission targets key sectors including healthcare, agriculture, defence, education, and climate science. Officials stated that; over 500 universities and research institutions across the country will gain access to AIRAWAT-2's computing power through a dedicated cloud-based portal, removing the barrier of expensive hardware for smaller institutions.

Speaking at the launch event, the Union Minister for Electronics and Information Technology said, "This is not just a machine — it is a statement of India's intent to lead the world in artificial intelligence research and innovation." Scientists and academicians from across the country welcomed the announcement, calling it a once-in-a-generation opportunity for Indian researchers to compete on a global stage. Students from engineering colleges, including those pursuing B.Tech in Computer Science, are expected to benefit directly through university tie-ups and research internship programs

The supercomputer will be operational for public institutional access starting 1 October 2026. Applications for research access are now open on the official NAIM portal. Experts believe this step will significantly reduce India's dependence on foreign computing infrastructure and position the country as a global AI powerhouse by 2030.

India's Logistics Sector Faces Reckoning as Digital Freight Platforms Reshape the Industry

'A new wave of technology-driven startups is challenging decades-old supply chain norms, promising faster deliveries and greater transparency — but questions about equity and implementation remain'

NEW DELHI, INDIA --    On a sweltering Tuesday morning at the Okhla Industrial Estate, Ramesh Choudhary guides his ageing Ashok Leyland truck through a tangle of loading bays, clipboard in hand, waiting on a phone call that used to take days to coordinate. Today, his booking came through an app in under eleven minutes.

"Before, I would sit idle for three, four days at the mandi waiting for a load," Choudhary, 48, said, wiping his forehead with a towel tucked into his collar. "Now I barely stop."

His experience reflects a broader shift quietly upending India's sprawling transport and logistics sector — an industry that employs an estimated 22 million people and underpins the movement of everything from mangoes to machine parts across the subcontinent.

"The truck is the same. The road is the same. But now someone knows where I am — and that changes everything."
            ~ Ramesh Choudhary, truck owner-operator, New Delhi

Digital freight platforms — apps and marketplaces that connect shippers directly with truck owners and drivers, bypassing brokers and intermediaries — have attracted more than $2.3 billion in venture investment across India in the past four years, according to industry tracker Tracxn. The platforms promise real-time tracking, dynamic pricing, and paperless documentation in a sector still largely run on phone calls and handwritten challans.


A SECTOR LONG OVERDUE FOR CHANGE ;

India's logistics costs have historically consumed 13 to 14 percent of GDP — roughly double the share in the United States or Germany — a gap economists attribute to infrastructure deficits, regulatory fragmentation, and an over-reliance on road transport for freight that more efficient markets would move by rail or sea.

The government's National Logistics Policy, launched in 2022, set an ambitious target: bring logistics costs down to 8 percent of GDP by 2030. Meeting that target, experts say, will require not just roads and ports but a fundamental rewiring of how cargo is booked, tracked, and billed.

"The inefficiency was never really about the trucks," said Dr. Kavitha Srinivasan, a supply chain economist at IIM Ahmedabad. "It was about information asymmetry. Nobody knew where anything was, and so everyone hedged."


WINNERS, LOSERS, AND THE BROKER IN BETWEEN ;

Not everyone is celebrating. Freight brokers — the agents who have for decades matched loads with trucks, taking a cut from both sides — describe the platforms as existential threats dressed up as progress.

"They say they are cutting out the middleman," said Suresh Agarwal, who has operated a freight brokerage in Kanpur for 31 years. "But who do you call when the app crashes and your goods are sitting at a toll booth at midnight? The algorithm won't pick up."
Labour groups have raised similar concerns about driver welfare. Several platforms classify drivers as independent contractors rather than employees, a model that critics say offloads risk — fuel costs, maintenance, downtime — onto the most economically vulnerable participants in the chain.

The Ministry of Road Transport and Highways did not respond to requests for comment on the regulatory status of app-based freight platforms. A spokesman for the All India Motor Transport Congress said the federation was "closely monitoring" developments and planned to present its concerns to the Union Commerce Minister next month .

For Choudhary, the philosophical arguments feel distant from the cracked asphalt of the Okhla loading bay. His phone buzzes — another booking, this one to Jaipur. He accepts it before the engine has had time to cool.
"The truck is the same," he said, swinging himself into the cab. "The road is the same. But now someone knows where I am — and that changes everything."
